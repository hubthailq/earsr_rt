\section{Published efficient SR models on small ear images}
\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{Effect of compression}
On clean AMI inputs all \NPub{} fidelity-oriented models exceed bicubic interpolation, by \AmiCleanGainMin{} to \AmiCleanGainMax{}~dB (Table~\ref{tab:published}). The gain shrinks quickly with compression (Figure~\ref{fig:sweep}): it is \AmiQHighGainMin{} to \AmiQHighGainMax{}~dB at JPEG quality 93, and at quality 85 and 75 every model is below interpolation, by \AmiQMidGapMin{} to \AmiQMidGapMax{} and \AmiQLowGapMin{} to \AmiQLowGapMax{}~dB. SPAN, for example, drops from \SpanCleanPsnr{} to \SpanQLowPsnr{}~dB while bicubic interpolation drops from \BicCleanPsnr{} to \BicQLowPsnr{}~dB.

The pattern holds across input sizes and datasets (Table~\ref{tab:generality} in \ref{app:generality}). At quality 75 all models are significantly below interpolation at the three AMI sizes and on AWEx; on EarVN1.0 \EarvnQLowBelow{} of the \NPub{} have a negative mean gain (\EarvnQLowSig{} significantly). At quality 93 no model is below interpolation. Under the measured degradation, which mixes the two levels, the published models are within \WildEstGainMin{} to \WildEstGainMax{}~dB of interpolation on the in-the-wild sets, against \WildCleanGainMin{} to \WildCleanGainMax{}~dB on clean inputs. The deficit does not grow as inputs become smaller (SPAN: \SpanQLowGapS{}, \SpanQLowGapM{} and \SpanQLowGapL{}~dB at 24, 36 and 48 pixels). Nor does it depend on the order of degradations (Table~\ref{tab:order} in \ref{app:order}). With the block grid offset from the image by 1 to 7 pixels, as when an ear is cropped from a compressed photograph, all \NPub{} models remain below interpolation at quality 75 (by \OrdShiftGapMin{} to \OrdShiftGapMax{}~dB; at most \OrdShiftDeltaMax{}~dB from the aligned case), and likewise after double compression (quality 93 with an offset grid, then 75; \OrdDblGapMin{} to \OrdDblGapMax{}~dB). Without chroma subsampling the gains change by at most \OrdChromaMax{}~dB. Compressing the large image before downsizing, in contrast, leaves little compression in the input, and all models are above interpolation (\OrdPreGainMin{} to \OrdPreGainMax{}~dB).


\subsection{What determines the sign change}
\label{sec:natural}
Our setting differs from the training protocol of these models in content, input size and compression. To separate these factors we evaluate the same models on the \NatN{} DIV2K validation images~\cite{agustsson2017div2k}, downsized to inputs of 24 to \NatLargePx{} pixels and compressed at the same levels (Table~\ref{tab:generality} in \ref{app:generality}). On natural images all models remain above interpolation at quality 75 and fall below it at quality 60, at every input size. On natural images, input size therefore has little effect on where the gain changes sign; on ear images the levels needed to locate the change were evaluated at two input sizes, 24 and 36 pixels, where it lies at quality \CrossQAmiMin{} to \CrossQAmiMax{} (Table~\ref{tab:crossover}). Content, in contrast, moves the change: the change occurs between quality 75 and 60 for natural images and between 93 and 85 for ears. Under compression the architectures also converge on natural images, from a \NatSpreadCleanS{}~dB spread on clean 36-pixel inputs to \NatSpreadQLowS{}~dB at quality 75.

\paragraph{Error decomposition} Let $E_i$ be the mean squared error (MSE) of bicubic interpolation on the clean input, $E_c$ the additional MSE of interpolation when the input is compressed, and $E_m$ and $E'_m$ the MSE of a model on the clean and on the compressed input. For each model and each of the \RatioCells{} combinations of dataset, input size and JPEG quality we write
\begin{equation}
E'_m = a\,E_i + b\,E_c, \qquad a = E_m/E_i ,
\label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}
so that the model improves on interpolation, that is, has a lower MSE (a higher PSNR) than interpolation on the compressed input, exactly when
\begin{equation}
(b-1)\,E_c < (1-a)\,E_i .
\label{eq:sign}
\end{equation}
Equation~\ref{eq:sign} only rewrites this comparison, and $a<1$ only restates that the model beats interpolation on clean inputs. What the measurements add is that $b>1$ in every combination and for every model: the model adds an excess $(b-1)E_c$ to the compression error of interpolation. The coefficient $b$ itself ranges from \AmpMin{} to \AmpMax{} and is largest under light compression, but only because $E_c$ is small there. The excess grows with compression for every model in every dataset and size group (\ExcessMonoModels{} of \ExcessModelGroups{} model--group pairs), and for the median model from \ExcessAmiHigh{} to \ExcessAmiLow{} times $E_i$ on AMI and from \ExcessDivHigh{} to \ExcessDivLow{} on DIV2K between quality 93 and 60, against a saving of \SavingAmi{} and \SavingDiv{} times $E_i$ (Figure~\ref{fig:excess}).

\paragraph{Where the excess lies} We split the excess of each model by pixel position and by spatial frequency, on 36-pixel AMI and DIV2K inputs (Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}); both splits add up to the excess of the whole image. At quality 75 on AMI, the excess per pixel is \MechBoundQLow{} times higher on input pixels next to a block boundary than on the others, so that these pixels, \MechBoundFrac{}\% of the image, carry \MechBoundShare{}\% of the excess. At quality 93, and on DIV2K at quality 75, the excess is nearly uniform within the block (ratios \MechBoundQHigh{} and \MechBoundDivQLow{}). Blocking therefore explains part of the excess, not most of it. In frequency, the compression error of interpolation lies mainly below the Nyquist frequency of the input (\MechAboveNyqEc{}\% above it on AMI), whereas the excess of the models is centred on that frequency: on AMI, \MechNearNyq{}\% of it lies between 0.6 and 1.4 times the Nyquist frequency and \MechAboveNyq{}\% above it (\MechNearNyqDiv{}\% and \MechAboveNyqDiv{}\% on DIV2K). The models add detail in the band they are trained to reconstruct, and on compressed inputs part of this detail follows the quantisation error rather than the image. These splits describe where the excess occurs; they are not a causal test.

\paragraph{Sign change} As a description of the \RatioCells{} combinations, which share images and are therefore not independent, the ratio $E_c/E_i$ orders them more consistently than the quality setting (rank correlation with the median gain \RatioSpearman{}, against \RatioSpearmanQ{}; Figure~\ref{fig:ratio}). The gain changes sign at a ratio of \CrossNatMin{} to \CrossNatMax{} on natural images (quality \CrossQNatMin{} to \CrossQNatMax{}), \CrossAmiMin{} to \CrossAmiMax{} on AMI (quality \CrossQAmiMin{} to \CrossQAmiMax{}) and \CrossWildMin{} to \CrossWildMax{} on the in-the-wild ear sets (quality \CrossQWildMin{} to \CrossQWildMax{}, coarse estimate from two levels; Table~\ref{tab:crossover}). The thresholds differ by about a factor of two and their intervals do not overlap, so there is no common threshold, but the ratio accounts for most of the difference between image types. Ear images are smooth: bicubic interpolation of a clean 36-pixel input reaches \BicCleanPsnr{}~dB on AMI against \NatSBicClean{}~dB on DIV2K. $E_i$ is therefore small, and at quality 75 the ratio is \RatioAmiQLow{} for AMI against \RatioDivQLow{} for DIV2K. The loss of the advantage is thus not specific to ears, but the compression level at which it occurs is, and the levels of real small ear images lie on either side of it.

\paragraph{Estimating the ratio without ground truth} Both terms can be approximated at test time: $E_i$ from the input itself, as the error of bicubic interpolation after downscaling it by two, and $E_c$ from the JPEG quality stored in the file. The estimate has a rank correlation of \SelRhoMin{} to \SelRhoMax{} with the true ratio. Used to choose, per input, between bicubic interpolation and the published SPAN weights, with a threshold selected on DIV2K, it gives a PSNR \SelCrossMin{} to \SelCrossMax{}~dB higher on held-out images of the three ear datasets than a rule based on the quality level alone, which selects the model at quality \SelLevelsDiv{}. Calibrated and tested on the same type of image, the two rules do not differ (\SelSame{}~dB on AMI). The ratio therefore transfers across image types where the quality level does not; the absolute gain is small (at most \SelGainMax{}~dB) and was measured on synthesised inputs.

These observations concern bicubic-trained $\times4$ models and JPEG compression; the only $\times2$ model, SwinIR-light, is consistent with them (ratio \XtwoRatio{} and gain \XtwoGainQLow{}~dB at quality 75, against \XtwoGainClean{}~dB on clean inputs).

\subsection{Architectures and perceptual metrics}
\label{sec:capacity}
On clean inputs the \NPub{} models span \SpreadClean{}~dB and larger models are better: the \ParamsRrdb{}M-parameter RRDB exceeds the \ParamsSpan{}M-parameter SPAN by \RrdbVsSpanCleanL{} to \RrdbVsSpanCleanS{}~dB. Under JPEG 75 they span only \SpreadQLow{}~dB, RRDB and SPAN differ by \RrdbVsSpanQLowM{}~dB, and the ranking is nearly unrelated to the clean ranking (Kendall's $\tau=\TauRank$). Among published bicubic-trained models, a different architecture does not recover the loss. Perceptual metrics tell a different story: at quality 75 \LpipsQLowBetter{} models have a lower LPIPS than interpolation (SPAN \SpanQLowLpips{} against \BicQLowLpips{}), since their outputs are sharper although less faithful, and BSRGAN reaches the lowest LPIPS (\BsrganQLowLpips{}) with a PSNR \BsrganQLowGap{}~dB below interpolation and \LatRatio{} times the latency of SPAN.
