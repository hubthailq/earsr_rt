\section{Discussion and limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What is new and what is not} That SR models trained on bicubic downsampling degrade on compressed inputs is known for natural images. What this study adds is, first, a rule for where the degradation begins: published models are better than interpolation while the error added by compression stays below about \RatioThresh{} of the interpolation error, whatever the dataset and the input size among those we tested. For smooth images such as ears this threshold is crossed at a compression (about quality 85) that lies between the two levels real images of the domain actually have, whereas natural images cross it only near quality 60. It adds, second, the observation that fine-tuning on the domain does not help unless the degradation is changed; evidence from identity labels that the effect holds on real small images and not only on synthesised ones; and the observation that, once the degradation is repaired, a \ParamsSpan{}M-parameter network suffices to exceed, on compressed inputs, every published model we tested in fidelity, including one \ParamsRatio{} times larger.

\paragraph{Limits of the claim} (i) The model is better than the published fidelity-oriented models and than bicubic interpolation on compressed inputs, in fidelity and in LPIPS. It is not better on clean inputs, and large GAN-trained models keep a lower LPIPS at a much lower fidelity and a much higher cost. (ii) The gain is explained by training with compression over the right range of levels (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}); we do not claim that estimating noise and blur is necessary, nor that the generic pipeline is worse for identification, only that it is \EstVsGenMin{} to \EstVsGenMax{}~dB less faithful and falls below interpolation on clean inputs. (iii) The threshold on the error ratio is empirical: it is observed for sixteen bicubic-trained $\times4$ models and JPEG compression on four datasets, and we have not tested other codecs, scales or training regimes. (iv) Fidelity results rely on synthesised inputs. The in-the-wild datasets have ground truth only at the smallest sizes, and the evaluation on real small images is through identification and viewer preference, not fidelity. The identification gain is established on one dataset (EarVN1.0: \RecSubj{} subjects with real small images, two recognisers of moderate strength and a control); its absolute rates are low and it should be read as a comparison between upscaling methods. On AWEx no method changes identification. The two conditions we propose for a benefit (compressed probes, and headroom between large and small images) were formulated after seeing that result, are confounded between the two datasets, and are supported within EarVN1.0 only by a split by file quality and a trend with headroom that does not reach significance. (v) AMI was photographed indoors and has 100 subjects; each fold is trained with one seed, so the spread across folds stands in for the spread across seeds. (vi) The realism classifier is evaluated on \RealTestSubj{} held-out subjects; an accuracy near 50\% shows that this classifier cannot separate the two sets, not that the distributions coincide. (vii) The blur of real small images is matched only through a sharpness statistic.

\paragraph{Practical guidance} For small ear images that have been through JPEG compression, a published efficient SR model should not be assumed to improve on interpolation. Fine-tuning on ear images is not sufficient if the degradation is left unchanged. Training with JPEG compression over the range of levels of the target images is sufficient and simple; a generic real-world degradation gives a similar identification rate at a lower fidelity. When the fine-tuning data come from a controlled acquisition, photometric augmentation and a brightness stress test should be part of the procedure.

\section{Conclusion}
Published efficient SR models are better than bicubic interpolation only while the error added by compression is small relative to the interpolation error. Ear images, being smooth, leave that regime at the compression levels real small ear images carry, and no choice among sixteen architectures changes this. Repairing the training degradation does: a real-time network fine-tuned with a compression-aware degradation measured from real small ear images, under a recipe that keeps fine-tuning stable outside the laboratory data, exceeds bicubic interpolation and the published models in fidelity on three datasets and nearly doubles rank-1 identification of real compressed small ear images on EarVN1.0, at no additional cost; where the input carries no compression, as on AWEx, it brings no identification benefit. The benchmark, the measured degradation and the baselines are released so that architectures designed for such inputs can be compared against a model that is trained correctly.
