\section{Discussion and limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the results add} The loss of bicubic-trained SR models on compressed natural images was known (Section~\ref{sec:related}). Our measurements locate it. The excess that every model adds to the compression error grows with compression, lies mainly around the Nyquist frequency of the input, where the models add detail, and overtakes the interpolation error saved at a compression level that depends on image content. It also occurs with WebP and AVIF, but the error ratio at the sign change differs between codecs: the condition of Equation~\ref{eq:sign} applies to all three, whereas a threshold on $E_c/E_i$ applies only within one codec. For ear images this level falls between the two quality levels most common in real small images. Under such compression the training degradation changes fidelity by more than a decibel and the architecture by a fraction of a decibel, both among published models and under identical training. Identification ranks the methods differently from PSNR: BSRNet, with a PSNR close to interpolation, has one of the highest rank-1 rates. On synthesised 24-pixel probes at JPEG 75, however, training with compression helps identification and bicubic training harms it, as for fidelity.

\paragraph{Practical implications} For compressed ear images, a published efficient SR model may not improve on interpolation, and fine-tuning on ear images does not help while the degradation is left unchanged. Training with JPEG compression over the quality range of the target images was sufficient; the range can be read from the image files. When fine-tuning data come from controlled acquisition, photometric augmentation and a brightness stress test prevent failures on in-the-wild images. Where inputs are not compressed, the published weights are preferable for fidelity, and on clean 36-pixel synthesised probes training with compression lowered identification. Where cost is not a constraint, large models trained with synthetic degradations identify real probes as well or better, with a PSNR close to interpolation.

\paragraph{Limitations} \emph{Model.} The adapted model is below the published weights on clean inputs, in fidelity and in the identification of clean 36-pixel synthesised probes, and it does not give the highest identification rate: BSRNet and Real-ESRNet exceed it with one of two recognisers. Under a smartphone CPU budget, or when fidelity matters, we would choose the adapted SPAN; with a neural accelerator and identification as the goal, a large model trained with synthetic degradations. \emph{Analysis.} The error-ratio analysis is empirical, covers bicubic-trained $\times4$ models and three codecs, and gives no common threshold across image types or codecs. Fidelity is measured on synthesised inputs, against ground truth that is clean only relative to the inputs, most clearly on EarVN1.0 (Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}); processing of real images beyond the variants in \ref{app:order} and \ref{app:codec}, such as resampling after compression, was not tested. \emph{Identification.} The gain on real probes was observed on one dataset (EarVN1.0, \RecSubj{} subjects), with low absolute rates and recognisers of moderate accuracy trained by us, whose rates are not comparable with benchmarks such as UERC~\cite{emersic2019uerc}; it halves with recognisers trained on upscaled images and was not seen on AWEx. The conditions we suggest for it were formulated after the AWEx result and tested only on synthesised probes, which are easier than real ones and were evaluated only with recognisers trained on large images, and the correlations between fidelity and identification are descriptive. \emph{Statistics.} AMI has 100 subjects, training variation was measured on one fold, the realism classifier is tested on \RealTestSubj{} subjects, and the smartphone latencies are medians.

\section{Conclusion}
Efficient SR models trained with bicubic downsampling add an excess to the compression error of their input. The excess grows with compression while the interpolation error they save does not, it lies mainly around the Nyquist frequency of the input, and it does not depend on the alignment of the JPEG block grid; it also occurs with WebP and AVIF, at thresholds that differ between codecs. Ear images are smooth, so the models fall below interpolation at compression levels typical of real small ear images. Under such compression, the training degradation matters much more for fidelity than the architecture. A published real-time network fine-tuned with JPEG compression over the measured range of quality levels, with a procedure that remains stable on in-the-wild data, improves on interpolation and on the published models on three datasets at no extra cost. It also raised the rank-1 rate of real small probes on EarVN1.0, by half as much when the recogniser is trained on upscaled images, but not on AWEx. On synthesised probes, models trained with compression helped at 24 pixels and JPEG 75 and lowered the identification of clean 36-pixel probes. The benchmark, the measured degradation and the trained models will be released to support work on architectures designed for such inputs.
