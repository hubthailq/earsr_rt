\section{Discussion and limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Relation to previous findings} It is known that SR models trained with bicubic downsampling perform worse on compressed inputs of natural images. This study adds several observations. For the published models we tested, each model reduced the interpolation error and amplified the compression error in all settings, and the models fell below interpolation when the compression error exceeded a fraction of the interpolation error between \CrossAllMin{} and \CrossAllMax{}, depending on the dataset. For smooth images such as ears this occurs at a compression level (quality \CrossQAmiMin{} to \CrossQAmiMax{} on AMI) that lies between the two levels most common in the real images we examined, whereas for natural images it occurs near quality 65. Fine-tuning on the target domain did not help when the degradation was left unchanged. Identity labels provided evidence on real small images that agrees with the results on synthesised inputs. Among the published models, which were trained with bicubic downsampling, a different architecture did not recover the loss under compression. At JPEG quality 75 on AMI the \NPub{} models differed by \SpreadQLow{}~dB, and a model with \ParamsRatio{} times more parameters was not better than a \ParamsSpan{}M-parameter model. Fine-tuning the latter with a degradation that includes compression gave a higher PSNR on compressed inputs than the published weights we tested. We did not train a larger network with compression, so our results do not show how network size affects the outcome when compression is included in training.

\paragraph{Limitations} The model improves on the published fidelity-oriented models and on bicubic interpolation for compressed inputs, in PSNR and in LPIPS. It does not improve on the published weights for clean inputs, and the large GAN-trained models have a lower LPIPS, with a lower PSNR and a higher cost.

The improvement is associated with training with compression over an appropriate range of quality levels (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}). We do not claim that the estimation of noise and blur is necessary, or that the generic pipeline is worse for identification in general. In our experiments it had a PSNR \EstVsGenMin{} to \EstVsGenMax{}~dB lower and was below interpolation on clean inputs.

We did not compare with a pipeline that removes compression artefacts with a separate network before upscaling~\cite{dong2015arcnn,jiang2021fbcnn}. Such a pipeline requires a second network at inference, and our experiments do not indicate how it would compare with training the SR model with compression on inputs of this size.

The analysis of the error ratio is empirical and does not give a common threshold. The ratio at the sign change differs by about a factor of two between datasets, the estimates for the in-the-wild ear sets are based on two quality levels, and the analysis covers sixteen bicubic-trained $\times4$ models and JPEG compression. We did not find a statement of this relation in the literature we searched, but the search was not exhaustive.

The fidelity results are based on synthesised inputs. The in-the-wild datasets provide ground truth only at the smallest sizes, and real small images are evaluated through identification. The improvement in identification was observed on one dataset (EarVN1.0, \RecSubj{} subjects with real small images, two recognisers of moderate accuracy and a control). The absolute rates are low, and the experiment should be interpreted as a comparison between upscaling methods. On AWEx none of the methods changed identification. The two conditions that we suggest for an improvement (compressed probes, and headroom between large and small images) were formulated after this result, cannot be separated with the two datasets, and are supported within EarVN1.0 by a separation by file quality and by a trend with headroom that is not statistically significant.

AMI was acquired indoors and contains 100 subjects. Each fold is trained with one seed, so the variation across folds also includes the variation across seeds. The realism classifier is evaluated on \RealTestSubj{} held-out subjects, and an accuracy close to 50\% indicates that this classifier does not separate the two sets, which does not imply that the distributions are identical. The blur of real small images is matched through a single sharpness statistic. The latency on the smartphone is reported as a median for six models.

\paragraph{Practical implications} For small ear images that have been JPEG-compressed, our results indicate that a published efficient SR model may not improve on interpolation. Fine-tuning on ear images was not sufficient when the degradation was left unchanged. Training with JPEG compression over the range of quality levels of the target images was sufficient in our experiments, and a generic real-world degradation gave a similar identification rate with a lower fidelity. When the fine-tuning data are acquired under controlled conditions, we recommend photometric augmentation and a brightness stress test.

\section{Conclusion}
We evaluated published efficient SR models on small ear images and on natural images under JPEG compression. In our experiments these models improved on bicubic interpolation when the error added by compression was small relative to the interpolation error. Ear images are smooth, and for them this was no longer the case at compression levels found in real small ear images. The choice among the sixteen published architectures had little effect under such compression, whereas the training degradation had a larger effect. A real-time network fine-tuned with JPEG compression over the measured range of quality levels, using a procedure that remained stable on in-the-wild data, improved on bicubic interpolation and on the published models in PSNR on three datasets, without additional computational cost. On EarVN1.0 it increased the rank-1 identification rate of real compressed small ear images from \RecRankBic{}\% to \RecRankEst{}\%. On AWEx it did not change identification. The small AWEx images show no JPEG block structure, and the recognisers lose much less accuracy between large and small images than on EarVN1.0. We examined these two differences after observing the result, and our data do not separate them. The benchmark, the measured degradation and the trained models will be made available to support comparisons with architectures designed for such inputs.
