\section{Discussion and limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the results add} The loss of bicubic-trained SR models on compressed natural images was known (Section~\ref{sec:related}). Our measurements locate it. The excess that every model adds to the compression error grows with compression, lies mainly around the Nyquist frequency of the input, where the models add detail, and overtakes the interpolation error saved at a compression level that depends on image content. For ear images this level falls between the two quality levels most common in real small images. Under such compression the training degradation changes fidelity by more than a decibel and the architecture by a fraction of a decibel, both among published models and under identical training. Identification ranks the methods differently from PSNR: BSRNet, with a PSNR close to interpolation, has one of the highest rank-1 rates.

\paragraph{Practical implications} For compressed ear images, a published efficient SR model may not improve on interpolation, and fine-tuning on ear images does not help while the degradation is left unchanged. Training with JPEG compression over the quality range of the target images was sufficient; the range can be read from the image files. When fine-tuning data come from controlled acquisition, photometric augmentation and a brightness stress test prevent failures on in-the-wild images. Where cost is not a constraint, large models trained with synthetic degradations identify real probes as well or better, with a PSNR close to interpolation.

\paragraph{Limitations} The adapted model is below the published weights on clean inputs, and it does not give the highest identification rate: BSRNet and Real-ESRNet exceed it with one of two recognisers, and compression removal before upscaling gives a comparable PSNR. The choice therefore depends on the budget and the measure: under a smartphone CPU budget, or when fidelity matters, the adapted SPAN; with a neural accelerator and identification as the goal, a large model trained with synthetic degradations. We found no evidence that estimating noise and blur is needed. The error-ratio analysis is empirical, covers bicubic-trained $\times4$ models and JPEG compression, gives no common threshold, and concerns fidelity, which for L1-trained models tracks identification; the correlations between fidelity and identification are descriptive and were computed after the identification results were known. Fidelity is measured on synthesised inputs. An offset block grid, double compression and the absence of chroma subsampling leave the results unchanged (\ref{app:order}), but other processing of real images, such as resampling after compression or other codecs, was not tested. The ground truth is clean only relative to the inputs, most clearly on EarVN1.0 (Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}). The gain in identification was observed on one dataset (EarVN1.0, \RecSubj{} subjects, two recognisers of moderate accuracy and a control), with low absolute rates, and the gain halves when each method has a recogniser trained on its own upscaled images; on AWEx no gain of the EarVN1.0 size was seen (the confidence intervals exclude only large effects), and the two conditions we suggest for an improvement were formulated after this result and cannot be separated with our data. The recognisers were trained by us, so their rank-1 rates serve to compare upscalers and are not comparable with published ear recognition benchmarks such as UERC~\cite{emersic2019uerc}. Finally, AMI has 100 subjects, each fold uses one seed, the realism classifier is tested on \RealTestSubj{} subjects, and the smartphone latencies are medians.

\section{Conclusion}
Efficient SR models trained with bicubic downsampling add an excess to the compression error of their input. The excess grows with compression while the interpolation error they save does not, it lies mainly around the Nyquist frequency of the input, and it does not depend on the alignment of the JPEG block grid. Ear images are smooth, so the models fall below interpolation at compression levels typical of real small ear images. Under such compression, the training degradation matters much more for fidelity than the architecture. A published real-time network fine-tuned with JPEG compression over the measured range of quality levels, with a procedure that remains stable on in-the-wild data, improves on interpolation and on the published models on three datasets at no extra cost. It also raised the rank-1 rate of real small probes on EarVN1.0, by half as much when the recogniser is trained on upscaled images, but not on AWEx, so the identification results hold under conditions that our data cannot fully separate. The benchmark, the measured degradation and the trained models will be released to support work on architectures designed for such inputs.
