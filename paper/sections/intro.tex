\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The ear can be photographed at a distance and without the cooperation of the subject, which makes it useful in surveillance and forensic settings~\cite{emersic2017ear}. In such images the ear often occupies a few dozen pixels and the file is JPEG-compressed: in EarVN1.0~\cite{hoang2019earvn}, several thousand ear crops have a short side of 24 to 48 pixels, all stored with lossy compression. Both factors reduce recognition accuracy~\cite{emersic2019uerc,rathgeb2016compression}. Enlarging such images, for an operator or for a recogniser, is a natural use of efficient single-image super-resolution (SR), whose networks run in milliseconds~\cite{ren2024ntire,ren2025ntire,ren2026ntire}. These networks, however, are designed and ranked on large natural images with bicubic downsampling and no compression. Previous ear SR studies~\cite{luo2014ear,zarachoff2018ear,markicevic2023ear} use uncompressed or undocumented inputs and do not evaluate efficient models.

SR models trained with bicubic downsampling are known to lose accuracy on compressed inputs~\cite{lugmayr2019unsupervised,chen2018cisrdcnn}, and training with compression is an established remedy~\cite{zhang2021bsrgan,wang2021realesrgan}. These works do not report at which compression level the loss occurs for efficient models, what determines that level, or whether it matters for real ear images. We measure all three with \NPub{} published efficient SR models, three ear datasets and a control on natural images.

The main finding concerns the error that compression adds. In all \RatioCells{} settings we tested, every model reduces the interpolation error but adds an excess on top of the compression error of interpolation. This excess grows with compression for each of the \NPub{} models in every dataset and input size, whereas the interpolation error a model saves is measured on the clean input and does not change with compression. The excess lies mainly around the Nyquist frequency of the input, where the models add detail, and only partly at JPEG block boundaries; offsetting the block grid or compressing twice does not change the picture. The ratio of compression error to interpolation error orders the settings more consistently than the JPEG quality, but the threshold differs between image types and depends little on input size. Ear images are smooth, so their interpolation error is small and the same JPEG quality gives a much larger error ratio (\RatioAmiQLow{} on AMI against \RatioDivQLow{} on DIV2K at quality 75). Accordingly, the gain changes sign at a much lighter compression: at JPEG quality \CrossQAmiMin{} to \CrossQAmiMax{} on AMI, compared with \CrossQNatMin{} to \CrossQNatMax{} on DIV2K. The two most common quality levels of real small ear images, 75 (\QLowShare{}\%) and 93 (\QHighShare{}\%), lie on either side of this point. At quality 75 all \NPub{} models are \AmiQLowGapMin{} to \AmiQLowGapMax{}~dB below bicubic interpolation, and the differences between architectures shrink to \SpreadQLow{}~dB.

Two consequences follow. First, the effect is visible on real small images without ground truth. Using the JPEG quality stored in each file and identity labels, we find that on EarVN1.0 the published SPAN weights improve identification only for lightly compressed probes (\RecQHighPub{} points at quality 90 or above, \RecQLowPub{} at 70 to 80), whereas a model trained with compression raises the overall rank-1 rate from \RecRankBic{}\% to \RecRankEst{}\%; when each method has a recogniser trained on its own upscaled images, this gain shrinks from \RecUpEarvnLgGain{} to \RecUpEarvnGain{} points but remains. On AWEx no gain of this size is seen; this dataset differs from EarVN1.0 in two respects that our data cannot separate. Second, under compression the architecture hardly changes fidelity, whereas the training degradation does: fine-tuning one real-time backbone with JPEG compression over the measured range of quality levels changes its PSNR by \DegEffMin{} to \DegEffMax{}~dB, and a network with \ParamsRatio{} times more parameters, fine-tuned in the same way, adds only \RevRrdbVsEstQMin{} to \RevRrdbVsEstQMax{}~dB. Identification ranks the methods differently from PSNR (Section~\ref{sec:recog}).

\paragraph{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
\item An error decomposition, measured for \NPub{} published efficient SR models: every model adds an excess to the compression error, the excess grows with compression whereas the interpolation error saved does not, it lies mainly around the Nyquist frequency of the input and only partly at JPEG block boundaries, and the compression level at which the gain changes sign is set by image content rather than input size.
\item A benchmark of small ear images with near-clean ground truth on AMI at three input sizes, two in-the-wild datasets that serve as further checks with less clean ground truth, and a natural-image control, with checks of the order of degradations (offset block grid, double compression).
\item Evidence that, under a smartphone CPU budget, covering the JPEG range of the target images during fine-tuning is sufficient and a larger architecture adds little. The adapted SPAN improves on bicubic interpolation by \EstVsBicMin{} to \EstVsBicMax{}~dB and on the published weights by \EstVsPubMin{} to \EstVsPubMax{}~dB on three datasets and runs in \LatPhoneSpanCpu{}~ms; RRDB, with \ParamsRatio{} times the parameters and the same training, adds \RevRrdbVsEstQMin{} to \RevRrdbVsEstQMax{}~dB.
\end{enumerate}

Results on the identification of real small probes are reported as conditional observations (Section~\ref{sec:recog}). We do not propose a new architecture, and the recipe relies on compression-aware training, which is established; its value here is that the analysis tells which compression range to cover. On clean inputs the adapted model is below the published weights (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
