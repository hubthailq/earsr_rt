\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The ear can be photographed at a distance and without the cooperation of the subject, which makes it useful in surveillance and forensic settings~\cite{emersic2017ear}. In such images the ear often occupies a few dozen pixels and the file is JPEG-compressed: in EarVN1.0~\cite{hoang2019earvn}, several thousand ear crops have a short side of 24 to 48 pixels, all stored with lossy compression. Both factors reduce recognition accuracy~\cite{emersic2019uerc,rathgeb2016compression}. Enlarging such images, for an operator or for a recogniser, is a natural use of efficient single-image super-resolution (SR), whose networks run in milliseconds~\cite{ren2024ntire,ren2025ntire,ren2026ntire}. These networks, however, are designed and ranked on large natural images with bicubic downsampling and no compression. Previous ear SR studies~\cite{luo2014ear,zarachoff2018ear,markicevic2023ear} use uncompressed or undocumented inputs and do not evaluate efficient models.

SR models trained with bicubic downsampling are known to lose accuracy on compressed inputs~\cite{lugmayr2019unsupervised,chen2018cisrdcnn}, and training with compression is an established remedy~\cite{zhang2021bsrgan,wang2021realesrgan}. These works do not report at which compression level the loss occurs for efficient models, what determines that level, or whether it matters for real ear images. We measure all three with \NPub{} published efficient SR models, three ear datasets and a control on natural images.

We first locate the loss (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}). For \NPub{} published efficient models, the median gain over bicubic interpolation changes sign between the measured JPEG qualities \CrossGridAmiLo{} and \CrossGridAmiHi{} on AMI ear images, against \CrossGridNatLo{} and \CrossGridNatHi{} on DIV2K natural images. The two quality levels most common in real small ear images, 75 (\QLowShare{}\%) and 93 (\QHighShare{}\%), lie on either side of this point, so whether a published model helps an ear image depends on how strongly the file is compressed. To explain the location, we write the error of a model on a compressed input as a multiple $a$ of the interpolation error on the clean input plus a multiple $b$ of the error that compression adds to interpolation (Section~\ref{sec:natural}). The decomposition itself is bookkeeping, but in our measurements $b>1$ for every model in all \RatioCells{} combinations of dataset, input size and JPEG quality. Each model therefore adds an excess to the compression error. The excess grows with compression and lies mainly around the Nyquist frequency of the input, while the interpolation error the model saves stays the same. Ear images are smooth, so the same JPEG quality gives a much larger ratio of compression error to interpolation error than for natural images (\RatioAmiQLow{} on AMI against \RatioDivQLow{} on DIV2K at quality 75), and the excess overtakes the saving at a lighter compression. The result concerns small crops that still carry their compression error; when the large image is compressed before downsizing, all models stay above interpolation.

We check this result with other orders of degradation, two other codecs, three input sizes, two in-the-wild ear datasets and natural images, and then test one practical consequence. For compressed inputs, fine-tuning a published real-time network with JPEG compression over the quality range of the target files is sufficient to improve on interpolation; for fidelity, and for the two network families we fine-tuned, the training degradation then matters much more than the architecture. On real small EarVN1.0 probes, with a recogniser trained separately for each upscaling method on images upscaled by that method, the fine-tuned network raises the rank-1 rate over bicubic upscaling by \RecUpEarvnGain{} points (95\% interval \RecUpEarvnGainLo{} to \RecUpEarvnGainHi{}), and a larger network fine-tuned the same way by \RecAllEarvnRrdbGain{} points, whereas published models that account for compression gain less, with intervals that include zero; on AWEx no gain is seen.

\paragraph{Contributions} Our main contribution is to locate the sign change and explain it. For \NPub{} published efficient SR models trained with bicubic downsampling, the gain over interpolation changes sign at a compression level set by image content, which for ear images lies between the two levels most common in real small images, and the reason is that $b>1$ in every combination we tested. Previous work showed that such models can fall below interpolation on JPEG inputs and that training with compression helps~\cite{chen2018cisrdcnn,zhang2021bsrgan,wang2021realesrgan}; it did not locate the level or relate it to content. Two further parts support it. We provide a benchmark of small ear images with near-clean ground truth on AMI, together with checks in which an offset block grid and double compression leave the result unchanged, WebP and AVIF also give $b>1$, and compressing the large image before downsizing returns all models above interpolation. We also show an application with its conditions, a real-time SPAN fine-tuned over the JPEG range read from the target files, which improves on bicubic interpolation by \EstVsBicMin{} to \EstVsBicMax{}~dB on three datasets and runs in \LatPhoneSpanCpu{}~ms on a smartphone CPU, and its evaluation on real small probes.

We do not propose a new architecture, and compression-aware training is established; the analysis shows when such training is needed and which compression range it should cover. On clean inputs the adapted model is below the published weights (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
