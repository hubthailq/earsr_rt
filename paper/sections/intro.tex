\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The ear can be photographed at a distance and without the cooperation of the subject, which makes it useful in surveillance and forensic settings~\cite{emersic2017ear}. In such images the ear often occupies a few dozen pixels and the file is JPEG-compressed: in EarVN1.0~\cite{hoang2019earvn}, several thousand ear crops have a short side of 24 to 48 pixels, all stored with lossy compression. Both factors reduce recognition accuracy~\cite{emersic2019uerc,rathgeb2016compression}. Enlarging such images, for an operator or for a recogniser, is a natural use of efficient single-image super-resolution (SR), whose networks run in milliseconds~\cite{ren2024ntire,ren2025ntire,ren2026ntire}. These networks, however, are designed and ranked on large natural images with bicubic downsampling and no compression. Previous ear SR studies~\cite{luo2014ear,zarachoff2018ear,markicevic2023ear} use uncompressed or undocumented inputs and do not evaluate efficient models.

SR models trained with bicubic downsampling are known to lose accuracy on compressed inputs~\cite{lugmayr2019unsupervised,chen2018cisrdcnn}, and training with compression is an established remedy~\cite{zhang2021bsrgan,wang2021realesrgan}. These works do not report at which compression level the loss occurs for efficient models, what determines that level, or whether it matters for real ear images. We measure all three with \NPub{} published efficient SR models, three ear datasets and a control on natural images.

We first locate the loss (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}). For \NPub{} published efficient models, the median gain over bicubic interpolation changes sign between the measured JPEG qualities \CrossGridAmiLo{} and \CrossGridAmiHi{} on AMI ear images, depending on the input size, against \CrossGridNatLo{} and \CrossGridNatHi{} on DIV2K natural images. The two quality levels most common in real small ear images, 75 (\QLowShare{}\%) and 93 (\QHighShare{}\%), lie on either side of this point, so whether a published model helps an ear image depends on how strongly the file is compressed. To explain the location, we write the error of a model on a compressed input as a multiple $a$ of the interpolation error on the clean input plus a multiple $b$ of the error that compression adds to interpolation (Section~\ref{sec:natural}). This decomposition is an identity; the finding is empirical: $b>1$ holds for every model in all \RatioCells{} combinations of dataset, input size and JPEG quality, and with WebP and AVIF. Each model therefore adds an excess to the compression error. The excess grows with compression and lies mainly around the Nyquist frequency of the input, while the interpolation error the model saves stays the same. Ear images are smooth, so the same JPEG quality gives a much larger ratio of compression error to interpolation error than for natural images (\RatioAmiQLow{} on AMI against \RatioDivQLow{} on DIV2K at quality 75), and the excess overtakes the saving at a lighter compression. The result concerns small crops that still carry their compression error; when the large image is compressed before downsizing, all models stay above interpolation.

We check this result with other orders of degradation, two other codecs, three input sizes, two in-the-wild ear datasets and natural images, and then test one practical consequence. For compressed inputs, fine-tuning a published real-time network with JPEG compression over the quality range of the target files is sufficient to improve on interpolation; for fidelity, and for the two network families we fine-tuned, the training degradation then matters much more than the architecture. On real small EarVN1.0 probes, with a recogniser trained separately for each upscaling method on images upscaled by that method, the fine-tuned network raises the rank-1 rate over bicubic upscaling by \RecUpEarvnGain{} points (95\% interval \RecUpEarvnGainLo{} to \RecUpEarvnGainHi{}), and a larger network fine-tuned the same way by \RecAllEarvnRrdbGain{} points, whereas published models that account for compression gain less, with intervals that include zero; on AWEx no gain is seen.

\paragraph{Contributions} Previous work showed that bicubic-trained SR models can fall below interpolation on JPEG inputs and that training with compression helps~\cite{chen2018cisrdcnn,zhang2021bsrgan,wang2021realesrgan}; it did not locate the compression level at which this happens or relate it to image content. This paper contributes:
\begin{itemize}
\item An analysis of the sign change. For \NPub{} published efficient SR models trained with bicubic downsampling, the median gain over interpolation changes sign between JPEG quality \CrossGridAmiLo{} and \CrossGridAmiHi{} on AMI ear images, depending on the input size, and between \CrossGridNatLo{} and \CrossGridNatHi{} on DIV2K natural images. The measurements explain this location: every model amplifies the compression error of its input ($b>1$ in every combination we tested), and smooth ear images leave little interpolation error to set against the excess.
\item A benchmark of small ear images with near-clean ground truth on AMI at three input sizes, two in-the-wild ear datasets and a natural-image control, with checks of the order of degradations: an offset block grid and double compression leave the result unchanged, whereas compressing the large image before downsizing returns all models above interpolation.
\item A fine-tuning recipe and its evaluation on real images: a real-time SPAN fine-tuned over the JPEG range read from the target files improves on bicubic interpolation by \EstVsBicMin{} to \EstVsBicMax{}~dB on three datasets, runs in \LatPhoneSpanCpu{}~ms on a smartphone CPU, and is tested by identification of real small probes.
\end{itemize}

We do not propose a new architecture, and compression-aware training is established; the analysis shows when such training is needed and which compression range it should cover. On clean inputs the adapted model is below the published weights (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). Taken together, the measurements support one main statement. \KeyFinding.
