\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The ear is an attractive biometric trait for surveillance and forensic use because it can be photographed at a distance and without cooperation, and its shape is stable over time~\cite{emersic2017ear}. In such footage the ear occupies a few dozen pixels. In EarVN1.0~\cite{hoang2019earvn}, a collection of ear crops gathered from the web, thousands of images have a short side of 24 to 48 pixels, and every one of them has been through lossy compression. An operator who wants to inspect such an image, or a pipeline that needs a larger input, must first enlarge it, and in an interactive or video setting must do so in real time.

Efficient single-image super-resolution (SR) appears to offer exactly this. A decade of work, driven in recent years by the NTIRE Efficient SR challenges~\cite{ren2024ntire,ren2025ntire,ren2026ntire}, has produced networks with a few hundred thousand parameters that run in milliseconds. These networks are, however, designed and ranked under one protocol: large natural images, bicubic downsampling by a factor of four, no compression. Inputs of a few dozen pixels that have been JPEG-compressed are far from this protocol in two respects at once, and the one prior study of SR for ear images that we are aware of~\cite{markicevic2023ear} does not address either \todo{confirm after reading the full text of that paper}. A practitioner therefore cannot tell whether a published efficient SR model helps on small ear images, and, if it does not, whether the remedy is a different architecture or different training data.

This paper answers both questions empirically.

\paragraph{What goes wrong} We build a benchmark of ear images with clean ground truth at three input sizes from the AMI database and add two in-the-wild datasets, EarVN1.0 and AWEx, for which ground truth can be constructed at the smallest sizes. We evaluate \NPub{} fidelity-oriented SR networks with public weights. On clean bicubic inputs they exceed bicubic interpolation by \AmiCleanGainMin{} to \AmiCleanGainMax{}~dB at 36-pixel inputs. Once the input is JPEG-compressed this advantage disappears: at quality 75 all \NPub{} models are \AmiQLowGapMin{} to \AmiQLowGapMax{}~dB \emph{below} bicubic interpolation on AMI and significantly below it on AWEx, and at quality 93 all of them are above it again on all three datasets (Section~\ref{sec:analysis}). The threshold thus lies between the two compression levels that together account for \QLowShare{}\% and \QHighShare{}\% of real small ear images. Perceptual metrics tell the opposite story at quality 75, where all models remain better than bicubic in LPIPS: the outputs are sharper but less faithful.

\paragraph{Where the bottleneck is} Under compression the \NPub{} architectures span only \SpreadQLow{}~dB, against \SpreadClean{}~dB on clean inputs, and a network with \ParamsRatio{} times more parameters is no better than a \ParamsSpan{}M-parameter one (\RrdbVsSpanQLowM{}~dB). Changing only the degradation used to fine-tune one real-time backbone, in contrast, moves its PSNR on compressed inputs by \DegEffMin{} to \DegEffMax{}~dB. The bottleneck is the degradation, not the architecture.

\paragraph{How to repair it} We measure the degradation of real small ear images directly: the JPEG quality from the quantisation tables stored in the files, the noise level with a classical estimator, and a mild blur chosen to match the sharpness statistics. A widely used generic real-world pipeline does not serve this purpose: a classifier separates its outputs from real small ear images almost as easily as plain bicubic downsampling (\RealGen{}\% against \RealBic{}\%), whereas the measured degradation brings it close to chance (\RealEst{}\%). We also report a failure that anyone fine-tuning on laboratory ear data will meet: a model fine-tuned on AMI alone produces noise on bright in-the-wild images (\CollapsePsnr{}~dB against \CollapsePub{}~dB for the weights it started from), and we give the two ingredients that prevent it.

\paragraph{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A benchmark and an analysis of efficient SR on small ear images: clean ground truth at three input sizes, subject-wise cross-validation, two in-the-wild datasets, and the finding that published bicubic-trained models fall below bicubic interpolation in fidelity under realistic compression, at every input size and on every dataset where the effect can be measured.
\item A degradation measured from real small ear images and a stable fine-tuning recipe, with evidence that the training degradation, not the architecture, limits quality on such inputs.
\item A real-time model, obtained without any architectural change, that exceeds bicubic interpolation by \EstVsBicMin{} to \EstVsBicMax{}~dB and a generic real-world degradation by \EstVsGenMin{} to \EstVsGenMax{}~dB on three datasets, together with an evaluation on real small ear images through identity labels (Section~\ref{sec:recog}).
\end{enumerate}

We do not propose a new architecture, and we state the limits of the result plainly: the model trades \EstVsPubCleanMin{} to \EstVsPubCleanMax{}~dB on uncompressed inputs for its gains on compressed ones, large perceptual models retain a better LPIPS at a fidelity far below bicubic, and most of the gain over a fixed-quality JPEG degradation comes from covering the range of compression levels rather than from the measured noise and blur (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).
