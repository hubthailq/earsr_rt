\section{Training with a measured degradation}
\label{sec:method}
We keep a published real-time backbone and change two aspects of its fine-tuning: the degradation of the training images (Section~\ref{sec:measured}) and the photometric range of the training data (Section~\ref{sec:stable}).

\subsection{Degradation of real small ear images}
\label{sec:measured}
We measure the degradation on the \FitSmall{} real small images (short side 24 to 48 pixels) and \FitLarge{} large images of the \RoleFit{} EarVN1.0 subjects reserved for this purpose.

\paragraph{Compression level} The quality setting is recovered by matching the luminance quantisation table stored in each file to the closest standard libjpeg table (the 100 standard tables are distinct; files with non-standard tables are assigned the nearest level). Its distribution is bimodal: \QLowShare{}\% of the images are at quality 75 and \QHighShare{}\% at 93.

\paragraph{Training degradation} The measured degradation draws, for every training sample, a JPEG quality from this distribution. When we specified it, we did not know whether compression alone would suffice, so it also contains small amounts of Gaussian blur ($\sigma\in[\BlurLo,\BlurHi]$ input pixels) and Gaussian noise, with ranges estimated from the same images (\ref{app:noiseblur}). The pipeline applies blur, bicubic downsampling, noise and JPEG compression with parameters drawn per sample; the generic pipeline has the same structure with wider ranges. Removing the noise and blur changes PSNR by only a few hundredths of a decibel (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}), so the component that matters is the compression range.

\paragraph{Comparison with real images} A patch classifier (\ref{app:realism}) separates bicubic downsampling and the generic pipeline from real small ear images with similar accuracy (\RealBic{}\% and \RealGen{}\%; 50\% means no separation), but the measured pipeline and bicubic downsampling followed by JPEG 75 much less (\RealEst{}\% and \RealJpg{}\%), which is consistent with compression being the main factor; the classifier is tested on only \RealTestSubj{} subjects.

\subsection{Stability of fine-tuning}
\label{sec:stable}
Fine-tuning on AMI alone improved validation PSNR on AMI but broke the model on in-the-wild images: on EarVN1.0, SPAN fine-tuned with bicubic downsampling reached \CollapsePsnr{}~dB on clean inputs, against \CollapsePub{}~dB for its initial weights and \CollapseBic{}~dB for bicubic interpolation. The failures occurred mainly on bright images, which are rare in AMI (\ref{app:stability}).

Two changes prevent this (Table~\ref{tab:stability}): a photometric augmentation, which with probability 0.75 multiplies the ground-truth patch by a random gain (log-uniform in $[0.7, 2.2]$), applies a random gamma ($[0.7,1.4]$) and scales each channel ($[0.9,1.1]$); and in-the-wild training images, which make up 30\% of the samples and come from the EarVN1.0 training subjects. A brightness stress test checks every run (\ref{app:stability}); all \NRuns{} runs reported here pass it, with a largest drop of \StressMaxDrop{}~dB in the margin over interpolation.

\subsection{Training details}
We fine-tune from the published weights for 20{,}000 iterations with the $L_1$ loss, Adam~\cite{kingma2015adam} with an initial learning rate of $2\times10^{-4}$ and cosine decay, batches of 64 input patches of $24\times24$ pixels, and an exponential moving average of the weights (decay 0.999). AMI training images are resized to a random short side between 96 and 192 pixels, so that one model covers the three sizes. We keep the checkpoint with the best validation PSNR; the seed equals the fold index, and a run takes about half an hour on an RTX~3080. The procedure is applied to two backbones of different families, SPAN with 48 channels~\cite{wan2024span} and DISP from the NTIRE 2026 challenge~\cite{ren2026ntire}.
