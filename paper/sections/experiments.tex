\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Setup}
For each backbone and each fold we fine-tune one model per training degradation: bicubic, generic, bicubic followed by JPEG at quality 75, and measured. All models use the procedure of Section~\ref{sec:stable} and differ only in the degradation. Together with the two ablation runs per fold of Table~\ref{tab:stability} and the two JPEG-only variants of Section~\ref{sec:ablation}, this gives \NRuns{} runs over the \NFolds{} folds. The models are compared with bicubic interpolation and with the published weights of the same backbone in four settings: AMI with 36-pixel inputs (\NAmiImg{} images of \NAmiSubj{} subjects), EarVN1.0 with 24-pixel inputs (\NEarvnImg{} images of \NEarvnSubj{} subjects), and AWEx with 24 and 36-pixel inputs (\NAwexSImg{} and \NAwexMImg{} images).

\subsection{Effect of the training degradation}
Table~\ref{tab:matrix} gives the PSNR of SPAN on AMI for each combination of training degradation and test input. Fine-tuning on ear images with bicubic downsampling improves the result on clean inputs, but under compression the fine-tuned model remains below bicubic interpolation by a margin similar to that of the published weights. In this case, training on data from the target domain did not compensate for the mismatch in degradation. The model trained with the generic pipeline is below bicubic interpolation on clean inputs in the four settings (by \GenVsBicCleanMin{} to \GenVsBicCleanMax{}~dB) and within \GenVsBicEstMin{} to \GenVsBicEstMax{}~dB of it on inputs degraded with the measured pipeline. It gives the highest PSNR only on inputs produced by the generic pipeline, which the classifier of Section~\ref{sec:measured} separates from real small ear images. On JPEG~75 inputs, training with the measured degradation improves PSNR by \DegEffMin{} to \DegEffMax{}~dB relative to bicubic training in the four settings, and training with fixed JPEG~75 by \JpgEffMin{} to \JpgEffMax{}~dB. These differences are more than ten times larger than the \SpreadQLow{}~dB range of the \NPub{} published architectures on the same inputs (Section~\ref{sec:capacity}).

\subsection{Comparison with the references}
Table~\ref{tab:main} gives the paired differences between SPAN fine-tuned with the measured degradation and each reference, on inputs degraded with the measured pipeline. The model improves on bicubic interpolation by \EstVsBicMin{} to \EstVsBicMax{}~dB, on the published weights by \EstVsPubMin{} to \EstVsPubMax{}~dB and on the generic pipeline by \EstVsGenMin{} to \EstVsGenMax{}~dB, and the confidence intervals of these differences exclude zero. \paragraph{Clean inputs} The model is adapted to compressed inputs. On clean inputs it is \EstVsPubCleanMin{} to \EstVsPubCleanMax{}~dB below the published weights, although it remains above bicubic interpolation (Table~\ref{tab:matrix}). None of the models we trained gives the highest PSNR for all types of input. Since the real small ear images we examined are compressed, we consider this trade-off acceptable for the intended use, and we report the results on clean inputs for completeness.

\subsection{Components of the measured degradation}
\label{sec:ablation}
The realism classifier did not separate the measured degradation from bicubic downsampling followed by JPEG at quality 75. Table~\ref{tab:levels} compares the corresponding trained models. Relative to the model trained with fixed quality 75, the model trained with the measured degradation is better by \EstVsJpgHighMin{} to \EstVsJpgHighMax{}~dB on quality-93 inputs and by \EstVsJpgMin{} to \EstVsJpgMax{}~dB on the measured mixture, and worse by \EstVsJpgLowMin{} to \EstVsJpgLowMax{}~dB on quality-75 inputs, which match the training of the fixed model. Training on the measured distribution of compression levels therefore gives better results across the levels that occur in the data, with a small loss at quality 75.

To separate the contribution of the compression levels from that of the measured noise and blur, we train two further models. The first uses bicubic downsampling followed by JPEG with the measured distribution of quality levels, without noise or blur. The second uses JPEG quality drawn uniformly from 60 to 95 and does not depend on measurements from the data (Table~\ref{tab:ablation} and the corresponding rows of Table~\ref{tab:matrix}). On inputs degraded with the full measured pipeline, the measured model is better than the levels-only model by \EstVsMixMin{} to \EstVsMixMax{}~dB. On inputs with the same compression levels but without noise and blur, the levels-only model is better by \MixVsEstOnMixMin{} to \MixVsEstOnMixMax{}~dB, and it is also better at quality 93 (\MixVsEstHighMin{} to \MixVsEstHighMax{}~dB) and on clean inputs (\MixVsEstCleanMin{} to \MixVsEstCleanMax{}~dB). Each model performs best on the inputs that match its training, so synthesised inputs do not show whether the measured noise and blur are useful. The identification experiment on real images in Section~\ref{sec:recog} does not show a difference between these models. The levels-only and the uniform-level models differ by \MixVsUniMin{} to \MixVsUniMax{}~dB on measured inputs, which suggests that the exact distribution of levels is of minor importance once the range is covered.

These results suggest that the main component is JPEG compression over the range of quality levels found in the target images, approximately 70 to 95 in our data. The measurement is useful for identifying this range, and we found no evidence that estimating noise and blur is needed. We keep the measured degradation as the reference model in all tables because it was specified before these comparisons were made.

\subsection{Identification on real small ear images}
\label{sec:recog}
The results above use inputs synthesised from larger images, because real small images have no ground truth. Identity labels provide a measure that does not require ground truth. We use the subjects of EarVN1.0 that were not used for SR training or for any parameter fitting (the test and reserved groups). For each subject, half of the large images (short side of at least 96 pixels) are enrolled and their embeddings are averaged into a template. \RecEnrolled{} subjects are enrolled with \RecGallery{} images. The probes are the \RecProbes{} real small images (short side 24 to 48 pixels) of the \RecSubj{} enrolled subjects for which such images exist. Each probe is enlarged $\times4$ by the method under test and resized to the input size of the recogniser with a fixed operation. The recogniser is a ResNet-18~\cite{he2016resnet} trained with a cosine-margin loss~\cite{wang2018cosface} on large images of \RecTrainSubj{} other subjects (\RecTrainImg{} images). It is not trained on small or upscaled images, which avoids a bias towards a particular upscaling method. As a control we repeat the experiment with frozen ImageNet features that were not trained on ear images.

Table~\ref{tab:recog} reports the rank-1 identification rate and the equal error rate (EER). With large probes the recogniser reaches a rank-1 rate of \RecRankRef{}\% (EER \RecEerRef{}\%), and its accuracy on held-out images of its training subjects is \RecValAcc{}\%. With real small probes and bicubic upscaling the rate is \RecRankBic{}\%.

Models trained with bicubic downsampling give rates close to that of bicubic upscaling. The published SPAN weights reach \RecRankPub{}\% (\RecPubVsBic{} percentage points relative to bicubic upscaling), and SPAN fine-tuned on ear images with bicubic downsampling reaches \RecRankBict{}\% (\RecBictVsBic{}).

Models trained with compression give higher rates. SPAN with the measured degradation reaches \RecRankEst{}\%, a paired difference of \RecEstVsBic{} points relative to bicubic upscaling and \RecEstVsPub{} relative to the published weights, and the EER decreases from \RecEerBic{}\% to \RecEerEst{}\% (\RecEerEstVsBic{}). The second backbone gives a similar result (DISP: \RecRankDispEst{}\%, \RecDispEstVsBic{} relative to bicubic upscaling). The differences among the compression-aware models are small. The fixed JPEG~75 and uniform-level variants do not differ significantly from our model (\RecJpgVsEst{} and \RecUniVsEst{} points), and the levels-only variant and the generic pipeline are about one point lower (\RecMixVsEst{} and \RecGenVsEst{}).

The GAN-trained models BSRGAN and Real-ESRGAN reach \RecRankBsrgan{}\% and \RecRankRealesrgan{}\%. The difference between BSRGAN and our model (\RecBsrganVsEst{} points) is not significant, and BSRGAN has \LatRatio{} times the latency on a desktop GPU.

With the control recogniser the rates are lower and the ordering is the same: \RecCtlRankBic{}\% with bicubic upscaling, \RecCtlRankPub{}\% with the published weights and \RecCtlRankEst{}\% with our model (\RecCtlEstVsBic{} relative to bicubic upscaling).

\paragraph{Second recogniser and second dataset} To examine whether these results depend on the recogniser or on the dataset, we repeat the experiment with a second ear recogniser, a ResNet-50 trained on the same data (\RecRfValAcc{}\% accuracy on held-out images of its training subjects), and on AWEx, which was not used for any training (\RecAwexEnrolled{} subjects enrolled, \RecAwexProbes{} real small probes of \RecAwexSubj{} subjects, same protocol). Table~\ref{tab:recogrobust} gives the paired differences from bicubic upscaling.

With the second recogniser on EarVN1.0, the rank-1 rate increases from \RecEarvnRfRankBic{}\% with bicubic upscaling to \RecEarvnRfRankEst{}\% with our model (\RecEarvnRfEstVsBic{} points), compared with \RecEarvnRfPubVsBic{} points for the published weights. With this recogniser the generic pipeline is significantly below our model (\RecEarvnRfGenVsEst{} points).

On AWEx the result is different. The improvement observed on EarVN1.0 is therefore not reproduced on AWEx.

\paragraph{Conditions for an improvement} The two datasets differ in two respects that may limit the contribution of an upscaling step. We examined them after obtaining the AWEx result, and we report them as possible explanations.

The first is compression. The small AWEx images show no JPEG block structure. The ratio of pixel differences across $8\times8$ block boundaries to differences inside blocks has a median of \BlockAwex{} for the AWEx probes, compared with \BlockEarvnLow{} for the EarVN1.0 probes stored at quality 70 to 80 and \BlockEarvnHigh{} for those stored at quality 90 or above. AWEx is distributed as PNG files and its compression history is not documented. A model trained to handle compression may have little to correct in these images.

The second is the difference between the rank-1 rate of large probes and that of bicubically upscaled small probes, which we refer to as headroom. It gives an indication of how much accuracy the recogniser loses at low resolution, and therefore of how much an upscaling step could recover. On EarVN1.0 the headroom is large (\RecRankRef{}\% compared with \RecRankBic{}\% with the ResNet-18 recogniser). On AWEx, where a subject has a median of \RecAwexGalMed{} enrolled images compared with \RecEarvnGalMed{} on EarVN1.0, it is close to zero (\RecAwexReRankRef{}\% compared with \RecAwexReRankBic{}\%). Within EarVN1.0, the improvement of our model tends to increase with the headroom of the subject. Among the \HeadEarvnReSubj{} subjects with at least five small probes, the half with less headroom (\HeadEarvnReLowRoom{} points on average) improves by \HeadEarvnReLowGain{} points and the half with more headroom (\HeadEarvnReHighRoom{}) by \HeadEarvnReHighGain{} points. With the ResNet-50 recogniser the two halves improve by \HeadEarvnRfLowGain{} and \HeadEarvnRfHighGain{} points. The rank correlation between headroom and improvement is positive but not significant with this number of subjects (Spearman $\rho=\HeadEarvnReRho$, $p=\HeadEarvnReP$, and $\rho=\HeadEarvnRfRho$, $p=\HeadEarvnRfP$). Headroom alone does not appear to be sufficient. The AWEx subjects in the upper half of headroom (\HeadAwexReHighRoom{} points) change by \HeadAwexReHighGain{} and \HeadAwexRfHighGain{} points with the two recognisers, although these per-subject values are based on a median of two probes.

In our data, upscaling improved identification when the probes were compressed and the recogniser lost accuracy at low resolution. EarVN1.0 satisfies both conditions and AWEx neither, so the two datasets do not allow the conditions to be separated. The separation by file quality and by headroom within EarVN1.0 provides the only within-dataset evidence for each condition.

In summary, on real small ear images that are JPEG-compressed, the results agree with those of Section~\ref{sec:analysis}. SR models trained with bicubic downsampling did not improve identification, with or without fine-tuning on ear data, whereas models trained with compression did, with two recognisers and a control. The choice among the compression-only degradations had no measurable effect on identification, which is consistent with Section~\ref{sec:ablation}, and the generic pipeline gave slightly lower rates in addition to its lower fidelity (Table~\ref{tab:main}). The improvement was observed on EarVN1.0 and not on AWEx. On AWEx the small images show no JPEG block structure and the headroom is much smaller than on EarVN1.0, and the two datasets do not allow these two differences to be separated. We therefore do not conclude that SR improves ear identification in general. Figure~\ref{fig:qualitative} shows examples.

\subsection{Efficiency}
\label{sec:efficiency}
The proposed training changes the weights of the backbone and leaves its computational cost unchanged. We define real time as a median latency of at most 33~ms (30 frames per second) for one $48\times68$ input, the largest input size considered here. On a desktop GPU (RTX~3080, 32-bit precision), SPAN with 48 channels (\ParamsSpan{}M parameters) requires \LatSpan{}~ms and DISP \LatDisp{}~ms, compared with \LatBsrgan{}~ms for BSRGAN (Table~\ref{tab:published}).

To measure latency on consumer hardware we convert \PhoneNModels{} of the models to Core ML with 16-bit weights and run them on an \PhoneName{} (\PhoneChip{}, iOS \PhoneOs{}), released in 2020, using the performance tool of Xcode, which reports the median prediction time on the device. Table~\ref{tab:latencyios} gives the results with computation restricted to the CPU and with all compute units (CPU, GPU and neural engine) enabled. SPAN requires \LatPhoneSpanCpu{}~ms on the CPU and \LatPhoneSpanAll{}~ms with all units, DISP \LatPhoneDispCpu{} and \LatPhoneDispAll{}~ms, and BSRGAN \LatPhoneBsrganCpu{} and \LatPhoneBsrganAll{}~ms. With all units enabled, Core ML assigned all operations of these three models to the neural engine. The two backbones used in this paper are below the 33~ms threshold on the CPU of this phone and require less than one millisecond on its neural engine. Among the three additional references, EDSR-baseline is below the threshold in both configurations (\LatPhoneEdsrCpu{} and \LatPhoneEdsrAll{}~ms), and RRDB, which has the same architecture as BSRGAN, gives similar values (\LatPhoneRrdbCpu{} and \LatPhoneRrdbAll{}~ms). SwinIR-light is above the threshold in both configurations (\LatPhoneSwinCpu{} and \LatPhoneSwinAll{}~ms); \PhoneSwinOpsCpu{} of its \PhoneSwinOps{} operations remain on the CPU when the neural engine is enabled. BSRGAN is \LatPhoneRatioCpu{} times slower than SPAN on the CPU, where it is above the threshold, and \LatPhoneRatioAll{} times slower on the neural engine, where it is below it. For inputs of this size, the real-time threshold is therefore also met by a large model on hardware with a neural accelerator, and the advantage of the small network on such hardware lies in fidelity and in the time left for other processing steps. The tool reports median values, so we cannot report a 95th percentile. The outputs of the converted models agree with the PyTorch outputs up to 16-bit rounding (PSNR between the two outputs: \CoremlPsnrSpan{}~dB for SPAN and \CoremlPsnrDisp{}~dB for DISP).
